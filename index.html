Mapping Drone

An autonomous 7-inch quad that flies survey grids on its own and turns the photos into maps and 3D models. It runs ArduPilot, a Raspberry Pi camera payload, and a ground station I wrote from scratch. Everything you see here is a 3D scan of the real drone, built from 69 photos. Scroll down to take it apart.

01 — airframe

Long-range bones, mapping payload.

The drone is built on a 7-inch long-range frame with deadcat arms. The front arms swing wide, so the camera looks straight down with no props in the shot. I designed the mounts that turn it into a mapping aircraft in Autodesk Inventor and 3D-printed them: the Raspberry Pi and battery mounts, a straight-down camera mount, and a 15° angled one for better 3D models.

02 — flight controller

Kakute H7, rescued.

The drone started on INAV, which can't run proper waypoint missions or trigger a camera by distance. So I moved it to ArduPilot. Partway through the switch, the USB-C connector tore off the board. I flashed the firmware anyway through the chip's DFU bootloader, and now every setting and calibration happens over the air.

The FlySky transmitter stays the manual override the whole time. It's the abort switch.

03 — companion computer

A Pi that does the photography.

A Raspberry Pi 4B with a Camera Module 3 rides along as the mapping payload. A service I wrote starts on boot and listens to the flight controller over serial. Whenever the autopilot decides it's time for a photo, the Pi takes a 12 MP shot and writes the GPS position, altitude, and heading into the file.

It also sends small previews to the laptop over WiFi, so I can confirm the shutter is firing mid-flight instead of finding out afterwards.

  1. Kakute H7 · decides when to shoot
  2. UART · MAVLink · 921600 baud
  3. Pi 4B · capture + geotag service
  4. WiFi · previews, telemetry, photo offload
  5. drone-gcs on the laptop

05 — ground station

It flies the survey itself.

I wrote drone-gcs, a ground station that runs on a laptop. You draw an area on a satellite map, and it works out the flight lines and photo spacing from the camera's optics. Then it uploads the mission to the flight controller and reads it back to check every waypoint.

The flight controller owns the whole mission, so a dropped WiFi link only costs the live map, not the aircraft. After landing, the photos come off the Pi and go through OpenDroneMap to become an orthomosaic and a 3D model.

06 — status

Where it stands.

The software is proven end to end in ArduPilot's simulator. The real aircraft is in bring-up:

  • Airframe & printed mounts
  • Ground station, proven in simulation
  • ArduPilot flashed over DFU
  • Pi ↔ flight controller link
  • Sensor, radio & ESC calibration
  • Props-off motor test & failsafes
  • Hover → Loiter → AutoTune
  • First real survey → map + 3D model

Lessons so far

  • Carbon fibre cuts WiFi range. So the system expects the link to drop out: the Pi saves everything to its own storage and fills in any gaps once it reconnects.
  • WiFi can deafen the abort link. The RC receiver is also 2.4 GHz, so the plan is to run the Pi's WiFi on 5 GHz.
  • Read the drift before fixing it. A steady one-way drift usually points to level calibration rather than the compass. I wrote flight-log analysis to tell the two apart once it flies.
